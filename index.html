You are not on a diet. You're training — and this is the long way on purpose.

Everything you've tried before was subtraction: eat less, cut this out, give that up. This one works the other way round — it's built on addition. More protein, more strength, more of what your body can do. You eat to perform. You lift so you can move the way you want to move on a court. The scale stops being a verdict on you and becomes an instrument, like a serve speed.

We're doing more than reframing this to sound motivating — we're going to change the actual programming. An athlete preparing for a season eats enough to train hard, protects muscle on purpose, and measures things to make decisions — not to feel good or bad about themselves. That's what the next six months look like.

The one sentence this whole program is built on: you like structure, and you want creative freedom inside it. So the structure handles every decision that doesn't need you — and leaves the ones that do.

What this is

Four things, one system

  • Food — a target, and an engine that builds your day around it so you never plan a meal.
  • Strength — 24 weeks of progressive lifting. This is the part that decides whether the weight you lose comes off your waist or off your frame.
  • Court — warm-up, the physical qualities pickleball actually asks for, and how to fuel a tournament day.
  • Head — the patterns underneath all of it. Taught in the moment it matters, not as a lecture.

They aren't four programs. Court play is why the lifting matters. Lifting is what makes the food work. The head stuff is what keeps you here in week fourteen.

What this isn't

  • A food list you're allowed to eat from.

The deal

Six to twelve months, and then the rest of your life

The commitment window is six to twelve months. Not because losing the weight has to take that long — although it can, and everyone's road is different — but because that's roughly how long it takes for a way of eating and training to stop being something you're doing and start being something you are. Short challenges work and then unwind. That's the whole reason for the timeline.

What you're actually buying is accountability and adjustment. Every two weeks we look at real data and change one thing. That's it. Everything else in this file is here so you don't have to hold it in your head.

Honesty policy

How to read the evidence tags

Anywhere this program makes a factual claim, you'll see one of these. They're not decoration — they tell you how much weight to put on it.

StrongMultiple good randomized trials, or a solid meta-analysis. Bet on it.
ModerateReal trials exist, but they're small, short, or in people not quite like you. Reasonable to act on.
ObservedConsistent patterns in populations, but nobody has run the experiment. Suggestive, not proof.
ReasoningIt makes biological sense and I'm doing it for that reason — but it hasn't been demonstrated. Labeled so you know.
Not trueCommonly repeated, and the evidence says otherwise.

If a coach, an app, or an ad can't tell you which of these applies to what they're selling you, that's information too.

Your numbers

Calculated, not assigned

Nobody handed you a plan number off a shelf. These come from your body, your training, and a floor set by physiology — and all of them move as you do.

The first thing you should know

How wrong this starting number could be

Say it plainly: any calculator has about a one-in-three chance of being off by more than 150 calories on your resting metabolism, and once an activity factor is added, the honest error bar on your daily needs is roughly ±500–600 calories. Strong

So this is not your metabolism. It's a starting hypothesis. Three weeks of your own intake and weight data will beat every equation ever published, and from your second check-in onward we run on your numbers, not the formula.

Expect the real number to land 200–400 calories away from this one, in either direction. That is normal. It is not evidence that anything is broken.

The kitchen

You don't plan meals. You are given ones that already work.

Every decision you make about food costs you something. Not calories — attention. By evening there's none left, which is exactly when the decisions get hardest. So this hands you a whole day of real meals, with the recipe and the shopping attached, and you overrule any of it whenever you feel like it.

How it builds your day: protein first, calories second. That's backwards from every app you've used, and it's deliberate — protein is what protects muscle while you're losing, and muscle is the entire point. Calories are just the envelope it has to fit inside. Strong

Every recipe feeds your whole household, with your portion marked. You cook once, everyone eats the same dinner, and you serve yourself the amount that fits your numbers. No separate sad plate.

Don't fancy something? Hit swap and you get a different meal that keeps your numbers intact. Hit recipe to see how to cook it and what to buy. Swap as many times as you like — that isn't cheating, it's the entire feature.

The evening

There is a slot for this on purpose

For most people the eating that breaks a plan happens at night. It usually isn't hunger. It shows up at the end of a day that had nothing in it for you — after the work, the cooking, the everyone else. That is not a willpower problem and it will not be fixed by a rule against it.

So the day is built with a real evening slot in it — calories and food you actually like, budgeted in advance, yours. Front-loading protein earlier makes it smaller. It doesn't make it disappear, and it isn't supposed to.

On the evidence: late eating does not cause fat gain independent of how much you eat in total. Strong Plans that ban evening eating fail because the eating happens anyway and then the whole plan feels broken. Reasoning

When you're out

No app, no scale, no numbers

You will be at restaurants. You'll be at your kid's tournament with a concession stand. Here's the whole system for those days, and it is deliberately crude:

  • Protein the size of your palm, at every meal. Two palms if it's the main event.
  • Vegetables the size of your fist, as much as you like.
  • Starch the size of one cupped hand.
  • Fat the size of your thumb — that's the oil, the butter, the dressing, the cheese.

Hand portions are genuinely useful and genuinely imprecise. They're worth about ±20%, which is close enough on the days that don't matter — and most days don't matter. Moderate

Strength

The part the old plan didn't have at all

Food is what makes the weight come off — there's no getting around that, and it's why the Kitchen tab exists. Lifting is what decides where it comes off: your waist, or the frame you need to play on. You need both. This is the half most programs leave out.

What it actually buys you

  • It protects muscle in a deficit. Diet alone loses roughly 5–6 lb of lean tissue for every 22 lb lost. Diet plus training cuts that by about half. Strong
  • Strength still goes up while you're losing. Lean mass gain is blunted in a deficit; strength gain is not. Strong
  • It is the best-evidenced injury prevention that exists. Across 26,000+ athletes, strength training cut injury risk by around two thirds and overuse injury nearly in half. Stretching did nothing. Strong in athletes generally Extrapolated to you
  • Loaded spine work builds bone. Heavy squats, deadlifts and pressing raised lumbar spine density about 2.9% over eight months in postmenopausal women. Moderate

What I'm not going to claim

  • Lifting does not build hip bone. In the trial everyone cites, femoral neck density rose 0.3% — statistically it beat the control group only because they lost 2%. It prevents loss there. It doesn't build. Moderate
  • No exercise trial has ever shown fracture reduction. Bone density is a stand-in. The likely real protection is that you get strong enough not to fall. Reasoning
  • That trial studied women averaging 65, and excluded anyone within five years of menopause — meaning you'd have been screened out of it. Applying it to you is reasoning downward in age. Sensible, not proven. Reasoning
The rules

Four guardrails

  1. Two sessions a week is the dose that produced every result above. Three is fine if your sport volume allows. Do not go past three while you're in a deficit and adding training.
  2. Add weight only when you finish every prescribed rep with clean technique on two sessions in a row. Not when you feel good. Not when the number looks small.
  3. Never progress load, training volume and sport volume in the same week. Pick one.
  4. You do not need to train to failure. Leaving one or two reps in the tank produces the same adaptations with less wreckage. Strong

Impact

The hip-bone problem, stated honestly

Bone responds to load where the load is. Heavy lifting loads the spine. Jumping loads the hip — roughly +1.5% at the femoral neck, from something like 50 low jumps a day spread out across the day rather than done all at once. Moderate

If you flagged a foot, toe or knee problem, this is a genuine conflict rather than a puzzle I can solve for you. Jumping is the stimulus your hip wants, and the sore joint is what has to absorb it. Options, in order of preference:

  • Flat, bilateral, low-amplitude hops in a stiff-soled or rocker shoe, which keeps the big toe out of the range that hurts.
  • Fewer contacts, more often — 10–20 a day beats 50 in one go, and spacing the jumps out actually increases the bone response anyway. Moderate
  • If it hurts, skip it and accept a weaker hip stimulus. A joint you can train on is worth more than a fraction of a percent of femoral neck density.

This is a footwear-and-podiatry question before it's a training question. A rigid forefoot or rocker sole does more for a stiff big toe than any exercise will. Reasoning

Sweat

Do this once, use it forever

  1. Empty your bladder. Weigh yourself with nothing on, before a typical session.
  2. Track every ounce you drink while you're going.
  3. Weigh again after, towel dry, nothing on.
  4. Sweat rate = (weight before − weight after + what you drank) ÷ hours.

Aim to keep losses under 2% of bodyweight. Do not drink past your sweat losses — over-drinking on a schedule is genuinely dangerous and it disproportionately affects smaller, slower participants. In heat, 300–700 mg of sodium per quart. Strong

The head

Taught in the moment, not in a module

Psychology in most programs is a chapter you skip. Here it's attached to the specific place it goes wrong, because that's the only time it's useful.

Pattern 1

Decision fatigue

Every decision you make in a day draws down the same account. Not a metaphorical one — the part of your brain that runs threat and urgency is small, it gets depleted, and micro-decisions drain it faster than big ones.

Which is why a plan that asks "is this okay? can I have that? do I need to swap this?" forty times a day doesn't fail on day three. It fails on day forty, when you've simply run out. And it doesn't feel like running out. It feels like not caring anymore. That feeling is the most reliable early warning sign there is.

What this program does about it: the engine makes the food decisions. The lifting is written down before you get there. Your evening is budgeted in advance. You spend your judgment on the things that actually need you.

Pattern 2

Every day is data

Here's the reframe that runs everything.

A day is not a win or a loss. It's a data point. That sounds like a nice way to feel better about a bad day, and it isn't — it's a mechanical claim about how this works.

The moment a day becomes a verdict, two things happen. You start avoiding the measurement, because who wants a verdict. And you start bailing early on things that were working, because a couple of bad readings feel like proof. Both of those corrupt the dataset — and the dataset is the only thing that tells us what to change at your next check-in.

A high day isn't a failure. It's information about a Tuesday. Ten Tuesdays is a pattern, and a pattern is something we can actually do something about. One Tuesday is noise.

The practical version: you never have to earn back a day. There is no compensating, no punishment session, no cutting tomorrow to make up for tonight. You log it and you move on. The engine handles the math.

Pattern 3

The evening isn't about food

You spend the day working, or buying and cooking and planning for everybody else. At night the house goes quiet and you want one thing that's yours. Food is available, immediate, and asks nothing of anyone.

That isn't a diet problem. It's an accounting problem — the day had nothing in it for you, and the balance gets settled at 9pm.

Three things that help, in order of how much:

  • A budgeted evening slot with food you actually like. Removes the transgression, keeps the ritual.
  • More protein earlier. Makes the pull smaller. Doesn't remove it. Moderate
  • Something else in the day that's yours. This is the one that actually solves it, and it isn't a nutrition intervention.

Sleep matters more here than meal timing does — short sleep raises next-day intake by a few hundred calories, which is larger than almost anything else on this page. Strong

Pattern 4

The scale is an instrument

Your weight moves 2–4 lb in a day from water, food volume, sodium, glycogen and sleep. Meanwhile you're losing about a pound a week of actual fat. The daily number is mostly noise. The trend is the only readable signal, and even one week of trend is marginal — two weeks is where it gets reliable.

That's not reassurance. It's math, and it's the reason a single reading is not allowed to mean anything.

Pattern 5

What you need to see to believe it's working

Some people live and die by the scale. Others need to see the shape of the thing improving — what they can lift, how they move in week ten versus week two, whether they're still standing in the third game.

Those are different people and they need different feedback. If the scale is your instrument, we watch the trend. If it isn't, we watch performance, and the scale becomes almost incidental.

Tell your coach which one you are. If you don't know yet, you'll find out around week six, and that's a perfectly good time to switch.

The truth tab

Everything I told you, and how sure I am

You're going to hear the opposite of some of this from people with more followers than your coach. Here's what the actual evidence says, including where it says "we don't know."

Things that are true

Load-bearing claims

ClaimGradeThe honest version
Resistance training preserves lean mass while you lose weightStrongRoughly halves the lean tissue lost per kilo of bodyweight. The single best-supported statement in this program.
Losing slowly protects muscle better than losing fastModerateIn the one direct trial, 0.7%/week gained lean mass while 1.4%/week lost it — same total weight lost.
More protein preserves muscle in a deficitModerateAbove about 0.6 g of protein per pound of bodyweight predicts muscle maintenance; below 0.45 g per pound predicts loss. But it did not translate into better strength or function, and it only works alongside training.
Strength training prevents injuriesStrong~68% reduction across 26,000 athletes. But those athletes were 12–40 and playing team sports; applying it to a 48-year-old pickleball player is reasoning, not proof.
High protein is safe for healthy kidneysStrong1,358 people across 28 studies. No effect on kidney function. This does not apply if you have existing kidney disease.
Protein doesn't harm boneStrongThe "protein leaches calcium" idea is backwards — the signal is neutral to mildly protective.
Daily weighing helps weight outcomes on averageStrongOn average. Averages are not people, and for some people it does measurable harm. Hence it being your choice.
Things that are not true

The list

You'll hearGradeReality
"Your metabolism crashes at menopause"Not trueTotal and resting energy expenditure are stable from about 20 to 60, across 6,400 people in 29 countries. The decline starts after 60, at under 1% a year. Menopause is not a step change. To the extent midlife metabolism drops, it's mostly lost muscle and reduced movement — both of which you can do something about.
"Processed food sits in your gut for months or years"Not trueMouth to toilet is roughly 1–2 days. Even in disease it doesn't approach a week. The colon lining replaces itself every 3–5 days; there's no surface for anything to stick to. The "mucoid plaque" in cleanse before-and-after photos is the psyllium and bentonite from the cleanse product itself. It has never appeared in any colonoscopy or autopsy in someone not taking the product.
"Your body spends 80% of its energy digesting"Not trueIt's about 10%. Protein is 20–30% of its own calories, carbs 5–10%, fat basically nothing. Going from a low-protein to a high-protein diet buys you roughly 45–50 calories a day. Real, worth having, not the reason protein works.
Cleanses, detoxes, gut resetsNot trueNo trial shows any commercial cleanse removes any named toxin. The literature is mostly case reports — including electrolyte disturbances and liver injury in healthy people, and products found to contain undeclared pharmaceuticals. The fair part: people genuinely feel better on one. That's real, and it comes from dropping alcohol, ultra-processed food and late-night eating for a week. You can have all of that without buying anything.
Parasite protocolsNot trueNot "the trials were negative" — nobody has run them, because the premise doesn't survive basic epidemiology. If you genuinely think you have a parasite, that's a stool test your doctor orders, not a supplement. Wormwood is neurotoxic at sufficient dose; these things are not inert.
"Adrenal fatigue"Not trueA systematic review of 58 studies concluded flatly that it is a myth, and no endocrinology society recognizes it. The fair part: real adrenal disease exists and real fatigue deserves a real workup — which is your doctor, not a $80/month powder. Chronic stress genuinely affects sleep, appetite and body composition. The problem is the fake diagnosis, not taking it seriously.
Apple cider vinegar for weight lossNot trueThe flagship trial behind every viral post was retracted in 2025 for improbable data. What's left is a small, low-quality glycemic literature in diabetic and PCOS populations. No credible weight-loss evidence. Also erodes tooth enamel.
Collagen for skinNot trueAcross 23 trials it looks like it works — until you split by funding and quality. Independently funded studies: no effect. High-quality studies: no effect. That pattern is the signature of a null result surviving on bias. Joints and tendon are a different question — modest, real-ish, worth a 12-week trial if you want. Don't count it toward your protein; it has almost no leucine.
Greens powdersReasoningThere's no trial of any commercial greens powder on any clinical outcome. Not harmful, not a scam — it's an expensive multivitamin with grass in it, delivering roughly one or two servings of vegetables and no fiber, for $60–100 a month. Buy vegetables. Watch the kelp content if you have any thyroid history.
Probiotics for weightModerate, and tinyAbout half a kilogram. For bloating in diagnosed IBS they're modestly useful — but only specific named strains at studied doses. "Take a probiotic" is meaningless advice. Diet outranks the pill.
Berberine, "nature's Ozempic"Moderate for lipids, no for weightUnder two pounds of weight loss, and the best-designed trial (337 people, 6 months, CT-measured) found zero effect on visceral fat. Genuinely useful for cholesterol markers, and notably more so in women. Safety: it interacts with statins, metformin and many other drugs. Doctor conversation, not a supplement-aisle one.
Seed cyclingNot trueEvery study is in women aged 15–49 with PMS or PCOS, nobody has ever tested the cycling part against just eating the seeds, and in menopause there's no cycle to time it to. Ground flaxseed is good food. The calendar is fiction.
"Metabolism boosters"Real but trivialCaffeine and friends move total energy expenditure 2–5%, and you build tolerance. That's maybe 100 calories at the optimistic end. Your protein target moves more than that.
"Eating late makes you gain fat"Not trueNot independent of how much you eat overall. What is true: ultra-processed food makes people eat 300–800 more calories a day — because it's soft, dense and fast to chew, not because of when it's eaten.
"You can only use 20–30 g of protein per meal"Not trueA measurement artifact. 100 g in one sitting produced substantially more muscle protein synthesis than 25 g, with under 15% oxidised.
"Protein within 30 minutes of training"Not trueThe effect disappears entirely once you control for total daily protein. The training effect lasts at least 24 hours.
"Menopausal women need a gram of protein per pound"Reasoning at bestNo trial in peri- or postmenopausal women shows a benefit at those intakes. Your needs are modestly up because you're 48, in a deficit and training hard — not because of estrogen specifically. The increment is smaller than the internet says.
"Cardio kills your gains"Not trueAcross 17 meta-analyzes, no meaningful interference for strength or muscle. And in women specifically, lower-body strength showed zero impairment. The one real casualty is explosive power, which is why power work goes early in a session when you're fresh.
"10,000 steps"Not trueA 1960s Japanese pedometer marketing number. About 7,000 captures most of the benefit.
"1,200 calories is the floor"Not trueThere's no trial identifying 1,200 as any kind of biological threshold — it's a micronutrient rule of thumb that hardened into a rule. Your actual floor is calculated in the Numbers tab, from your body and your training, and it's considerably higher.
"Exercise prevents fractures"Not demonstratedNo exercise trial has ever been powered for fracture outcomes. Bone density is a surrogate. The likely real mechanism is that strong people don't fall — and the strength gains in these trials dwarf the bone gains.
"Exercise fixes hot flushes"InsufficientCochrane, 5 trials, 733 women: evidence insufficient to say whether it helps.
The short list

Supplements actually worth the money

  • Creatine monohydrate, 3–5 g a day. No loading, timing irrelevant, the cheapest well-evidenced thing in the category. It does not make women bulky, it does not cause hair loss, and it does not harm healthy kidneys. Strong for muscle Do not buy it for bone — a two-year trial in postmenopausal women found no bone benefit over lifting alone. Moderate, negative
  • Vitamin D, if your bloodwork says you're low. If it doesn't, it does nothing. Very high doses appear to be actively worse for bone in women, so more is not better here. Moderate
  • Protein powder, if it's the difference between hitting your protein and not. It has no magic in it — it's a convenient delivery vehicle for the number in the Numbers tab.
  • Creatine and protein are the entire list. Everything else is either "if your labs say so" or a conversation with your doctor.
Where this program is guessing

The honest gaps

  • There is no trial of heavy lifting and impact work in perimenopausal women in their late forties. Everything in the Strength tab reasons downward in age from trials in women 60+. That reasoning is sound. It is not proof.
  • The 24-week structure is professional judgment, assembled from evidence about its parts. Nobody has tested this exact program.
  • Per-meal protein targets are a tool for hitting the daily number, not an independent requirement. The head-to-head trial comparing even versus skewed distribution found no difference at all.
  • Reverse dieting has essentially no evidence base. The recovery phase at the end of this program is built on reasoning about adherence and psychology, and it's labeled that way rather than dressed up as metabolic protection.
  • Nobody has tested any injury-prevention program in pickleball. Not one.

If that list makes you trust the program less, good — that's the correct response to being told what somebody doesn't know. It's also the reason the check-in engine exists: when the evidence can't tell us, your own data can.
